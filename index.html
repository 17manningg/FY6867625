Introduction

    Dams are engineered to control water flow, creating reservoirs for irrigation, flood control, and power generation. 
    Hydropower generates over 16% of the world’s electricity, with more than 150 countries contributing to its production (Gernaat, et al., 2017).
    Yet, nearly half of all hydropower comes from only four countries: China, Brazil, Canada, and the United States.
    China produces the most hydropower, with its’ output having tripled in the last decade due to the construction of some of the largest dams ever built.
    See Figure 1 below (International Hydropower Association, 2022) While hydropower is an important source of energy, its environmental impacts are significant.
    One famous environmentalist who was commenting on the construction of 30,000 dams across the American West, stated, “They dewatered countless rivers,
    destroyed millions of acres of riparian habitat, blocked thousands of miles of salmon spawning grounds, silted over breeding beds,
    polluted return flows with agricultural chemicals, and unleashed the plague of livestock on arid lands—ultimately making survival impossible for many native species” (Reisner, 2017)
    The environmental costs of dams often outweigh their benefits. This report examines and shows how detrimental these dams can be on wildlife,
    causing irreversible  damage to the habitats and species surrounding the rivers and streams located around the dams.

Positive Environmental Impacts of Dams

    Dams can have positive environmental impacts when planned and managed well. They help to control water flow, which reduces the risk of both floods and droughts,
    and provides a steady water supply that supports ecosystems and wetlands. By generating clean, renewable hydropower, dams also contribute to food and fish production,
    drinking water and sanitation. Reservoirs created by dams can serve as habitats for fish, birds, and other wildlife. (International Commission on Irrigation and Drainage, 1999-2000).
    Dams also allow for irrigation and reforestation, especially in dry areas, which increases biodiversity and can contribute to increased storage of carbon (Boix-Fayos, et al., 2007).
