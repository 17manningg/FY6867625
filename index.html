Introduction

    Dams are engineered to control water flow, creating reservoirs for irrigation, flood control, and power generation. 
    Hydropower generates over 16% of the world’s electricity, with more than 150 countries contributing to its production (Gernaat, et al., 2017).
    Yet, nearly half of all hydropower comes from only four countries: China, Brazil, Canada, and the United States.
    China produces the most hydropower, with its’ output having tripled in the last decade due to the construction of some of the largest dams ever built.
    See Figure 1 below (International Hydropower Association, 2022) While hydropower is an important source of energy, its environmental impacts are significant.
    One famous environmentalist who was commenting on the construction of 30,000 dams across the American West, stated, “They dewatered countless rivers,
    destroyed millions of acres of riparian habitat, blocked thousands of miles of salmon spawning grounds, silted over breeding beds,
    polluted return flows with agricultural chemicals, and unleashed the plague of livestock on arid lands—ultimately making survival impossible for many native species” (Reisner, 2017)
    The environmental costs of dams often outweigh their benefits. This report examines and shows how detrimental these dams can be on wildlife,
    causing irreversible  damage to the habitats and species surrounding the rivers and streams located around the dams.

Positive Environmental Impacts of Dams

    Dams can have positive environmental impacts when planned and managed well. They help to control water flow, which reduces the risk of both floods and droughts,
    and provides a steady water supply that supports ecosystems and wetlands. By generating clean, renewable hydropower, dams also contribute to food and fish production,
    drinking water and sanitation. Reservoirs created by dams can serve as habitats for fish, birds, and other wildlife. (International Commission on Irrigation and Drainage, 1999-2000).
    Dams also allow for irrigation and reforestation, especially in dry areas, which increases biodiversity and can contribute to increased storage of carbon (Boix-Fayos, et al., 2007).

Negative Impacts on Aquatic Ecosystems


    Dams can have serious negative effects on aquatic ecosystems, disrupting natural processes and threatening biodiversity (Wu, et al., 2019).
    One of the biggest issues is how they change the natural flow of rivers. Reduced downstream flow can dry up habitats and shrink floodplains that many species rely on for breeding and feeding.
    Dams can also create physical barriers that prevent fish, such as salmon, from reaching their spawning grounds, which results in reduced fish populations (NOAA Fisheries, 2023).
    The quality of the water can be affected by dams. Sediments that would naturally flow downstream can be trapped, preventing nutrients from reaching downstream habitats.
    Reservoirs can experience temperature changes, oxygen depletion, and nutrient buildup, all of which can harm aquatic life. These conditions often lead to algal blooms which can lead to
    areas where aquatic life cannot survive (Soomro, et al., 2023). While dams provide important benefits for people, it is essential to address their ecological impacts to protect aquatic
    environments and the species that depend on them.

Impact on Terrestrial Ecosystems


    Dams have significant impacts on terrestrial ecosystems, largely through habitat loss. The creation of reservoirs means that large areas of land are flooded, submerging forests, grasslands,
    and other habitats, which displaces wildlife and disrupts ecosystems. The submergence of vegetation results in the decay of plants, releasing greenhouse gases like methane and carbon dioxide,
    which contributes to climate change (ScienceAdviser, 2016). Dams also disrupt local microclimates, altering humidity and temperature patterns,
    which can affect plant growth and animal habitats (earth LAW CENTER, 2017). Agricultural land can be lost due to flooding, forcing people to clear new areas for farming, often leading to deforestation.
    Communities displaced by dams may further cause additional ecological damage as they move to new lands. Overall, the construction of dams has profound and often irreversible effects on terrestrial ecosystems,
    requiring careful management to reduce the environmental consequences.

Human and Climate-Related Impacts

    Dams have significant impacts on both people and the climate. The construction of dams often means that whole communities can be forced out of large areas of land when the lands are flooded to create reservoirs,
    leading to the loss of homes and farmland. Agriculture can suffer as fertile lands are submerged, and downstream water availability is reduced, reducing access to water for communities downstream. River flow and
    sediment transport disrupted by dams can also reduce the fertility of downstream agricultural lands, risking food security for those communities who rely on floodplain farming (ENVIROTECH, 2024). Reservoirs can
    release significant amounts of methane and carbon dioxide due to submerged vegetation decomposing without oxygen, especially in warmer regions. This makes some dams as environmentally harmful as fossil fuels.
    Balancing consequences with the benefits of dams requires careful consideration.

Mitigation Strategies and Sustainable Solutions


    Mitigating the environmental and social impacts of dams requires careful management. One approach is to integrate fish ladders or bypass systems to restore connectivity and allow migratory species to access breeding grounds (Silva, et al., 2018).
    Sediment management is another solution, such as releasing controlled water flows to transport sediments downstream, replenishing ecosystems and deltas (Rheinheimer & Yarnell, 2017). To minimise greenhouse gas emissions,
    clearing vegetation before flooding reservoirs reduces methane production, particularly in tropical regions. Additionally, small-scale hydropower projects or run-of-river systems, which do not require large reservoirs,
    are more sustainable alternatives. See Figure 2 (Paish, 2002).

Conclusion


    Dams are useful for managing water resources, generating energy, and supporting human development, but they come with significant environmental, social, and climate-related challenges. Addressing these impacts requires careful planning,
    sustainable design, and mitigation strategies to balance human needs with the environment and wildlife. Innovative solutions like fish ladders and sediment management, can help to reduce the negative effects of dams and create systems that
    benefit both people and the environment. The future of dams lies in prioritizing wildlife and to not further damage important ecosystems, as dams have both positives as well as negatives humans have to learn to adapt to protect the environment
    while maintaining them.
